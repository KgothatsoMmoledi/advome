IN-PERSON HEARING AT CCMA OFFICES

Prepare for Your Arbitration

Conciliation failed. You have a Certificate of Non-Resolution. Now you face a formal hearing where a commissioner will decide your case. This is the most important stage.

What is Arbitration?

Arbitration is a formal legal hearing — much more serious than conciliation. Think of it as a simplified court trial:

A Real Hearing

You and your employer present evidence under oath. Witnesses testify. The commissioner acts like a judge.

Binding Decision

The commissioner issues a written award deciding who wins and who loses. The award is legally binding and enforceable.

Legal Representation Allowed

Unlike conciliation, you may bring an attorney or representative. But many people still self-represent successfully.

14-Day Award

The commissioner must issue the written award within 14 days of the hearing ending. It is enforceable as a Labour Court order.

CCMA is IN-PERSON — Not Online

Arbitration hearings are held physically at CCMA offices unless specifically arranged otherwise. You must attend in person. Bring your ID. Dress professionally. This is a courtroom-style setup — formal seating, the commissioner at the head, parties on opposite sides. Do not treat this like a casual meeting.

Con-Arb Warning

Same-Day Arbitration Possible

If con-arb (conciliation-arbitration) was not objected to when you filed your referral, arbitration may follow immediately if conciliation fails today. This means you could go straight from settlement talks into a formal hearing on the same day.

Be Prepared for Both:

  • Bring all evidence and witnesses as if arbitration is happening today
  • Dress and prepare for a formal hearing, not just a chat
  • If you are not ready for con-arb, you may request a postponement, but this is not guaranteed
  • Employers often object to con-arb — check your set-down notice carefully
Not a Substitute for an AttorneyIf you are facing con-arb and are unprepared, strongly consider requesting a postponement or consulting an attorney. Advome does not provide legal representation.

What to Bring — Checklist

Arbitration requires complete preparation. Missing documents can weaken your case. Use this checklist:

  • Original ID DocumentRequired for security and verification. Passport, driver's license, or smart ID card.
  • CCMA Set-Down NoticeThe official notice with your case number, date, time, and venue. You received this after requesting arbitration.
  • Employment Contract & Job DescriptionProves your terms of employment, role, and responsibilities.
  • Payslips & Proof of Earnings (Last 3–6 months)Critical for calculating compensation or reinstatement back pay.
  • Dismissal Letter / Termination NoticeThe official document stating why you were dismissed. If verbal, bring any follow-up emails or messages.
  • Disciplinary Records & WarningsAny prior warnings, disciplinary hearing minutes, or performance improvement plans.
  • ALL Evidence Supporting Your CaseEmails, WhatsApp messages, photos, videos, timesheets, attendance records — everything relevant.
  • Witness Statements (Signed & Dated)Written statements from colleagues who saw what happened. Include their full names, contact numbers, and what they will testify about.
  • Witness Contact DetailsPhone numbers and addresses for all witnesses. The commissioner may need to contact them.
  • Evidence Bundle (Numbered, Indexed, 3 Copies)Organize all documents into a bundle with page numbers and an index. Bring one copy for you, one for the commissioner, and one for the employer.
  • Pen, Paper & NotepadTake notes during the hearing. Write down questions, objections, and the employer's arguments.
  • Water & SnacksArbitration can take hours. Stay hydrated and maintain your energy.
  • Recording Device (Phone with voice recorder)Under South African law, you may record proceedings you are part of for personal records. See recording section below.

Pro Tip: The "Arbitration Bundle"

Your evidence bundle is your most important tool. Organize it as: (1) Index, (2) Chronology of events, (3) Employment documents, (4) Dismissal documents, (5) Supporting evidence, (6) Witness statements, (7) Legal arguments summary. Number every page. Use dividers. The commissioner and employer each get an identical copy.

How to Behave at Arbitration

Arbitration is more formal than conciliation. The commissioner has authority to control the hearing. Your conduct affects their impression of your credibility.

Professional Dress & Conduct

Dress neatly (business casual minimum). Address the commissioner as "Commissioner" or "Sir/Madam." Stand when the commissioner enters the room. Turn your phone off completely. Do not interrupt when others are speaking.

Speaking Only When Called Upon

Unlike conciliation, you do not speak freely. The commissioner controls who speaks when. Wait to be asked. When testifying, speak clearly and directly answer the question. Do not argue with the employer during their presentation.

Emotional Management Under Pressure

Cross-examination can feel aggressive. The employer's representative may challenge your honesty. Stay calm. If you feel overwhelmed, ask the commissioner for a short break. Take deep breaths. Do not shout, cry, or walk out.

Cross-Examination Etiquette

When the employer questions you: listen carefully, answer truthfully, and do not guess. If you don't know, say "I don't know." If you don't understand a question, ask for clarification. Never lie. Perjury is a criminal offence.

What NOT to Do

Do not threaten the employer, commissioner, or witnesses. Do not bring unauthorized people into the hearing room. Do not discuss the case in public waiting areas. Do not falsify documents or lie under oath. Do not refuse to answer questions without legal grounds. Do not record secretly if the commissioner has prohibited it.

Representation RulesAt arbitration, you may appear in person or be represented by a co-employee, trade union official, employer's organisation official, or legal practitioner (attorney/advocate). Advome is not a law firm and cannot represent you at the CCMA.

What to Expect During the Hearing

1

Opening Statements

The commissioner introduces themselves and explains the procedure. Both parties give brief opening statements (5–10 minutes) outlining what they will prove. State the facts simply: what happened, when, and what you want. Do not argue — just outline your case.

2

Leading Evidence (Your Case)

You present your evidence first (since you are the applicant). You may testify yourself and call witnesses. For each witness: you ask questions first (examination-in-chief), then the employer cross-examines, then you may re-examine.

3

Witness Testimony

Each witness takes an oath or affirmation to tell the truth. They tell their story, then face cross-examination from the other side, then brief re-examination from you. Prepare your witnesses thoroughly — they must know what they can and cannot say.

4

Employer's Case

The employer presents their evidence and witnesses. You have the right to cross-examine their witnesses. Listen carefully and take notes. Look for contradictions, missing documents, or witnesses who cannot support the employer's version.

5

Commissioner's Questions

The commissioner may ask questions of any witness at any time. They are trying to understand the facts. Answer directly and respectfully. The commissioner is not against you — they are gathering information to make a fair decision.

6

Closing Arguments

After all evidence is presented, both parties make closing arguments. Summarize the key facts, highlight the evidence that supports your case, and explain why the law supports your position. Do not introduce new evidence at this stage.

Recording the Arbitration

Single-Party Consent (South African Law)

Under South African law, you may record a conversation you are part of without telling the other party, for your personal records. This is called "single-party consent." However, arbitration is a formal legal proceeding with different etiquette.

What You CAN Do

Record the proceedings for your personal memory aid. Use your phone's voice recorder app. Place it on the table in front of you — do not hide it. The recording helps you remember what was said, especially for a potential review.

What You SHOULD NOT Do

Do not record secretly if the commissioner has prohibited recording. Do not publish or share the recording online. Do not use it to intimidate anyone. The recording is for your personal legal records only.

CCMA Recording Equipment

The CCMA typically records all arbitration proceedings digitally. You may request a copy of the official recording from the CCMA office after the hearing. This is the preferred record for any Labour Court review.

For Review Purposes

If you plan to challenge the award in the Labour Court, you will need the official CCMA recording (or transcript) as part of the review record. Your personal recording is a backup, not the official record.

Evidence Rules — Explained Simply

Arbitration has formal evidence rules. You do not need to be a lawyer, but you must understand the basics:

Relevance

Only evidence that relates directly to your dispute is allowed. The commissioner will stop you if you bring up unrelated matters. Stick to: your employment, your dismissal, and the fairness of both the procedure and the reason.

Authenticity

Documents must be genuine. If the employer disputes a document, you may need to prove it is real. Bring original documents where possible. Certified copies are acceptable for most purposes.

Hearsay

Hearsay is when you repeat what someone else said, and that person is not present to testify. Generally, hearsay is not allowed. However, there are exceptions — ask the commissioner if you are unsure.

Documents Under Oath

When you testify, you swear an oath to tell the truth. Everything you say is under oath. If you present documents, you may need to confirm they are true. Lying under oath is perjury — a criminal offence.

Bundle Preparation Tips

Number every page from 1 to the end. Create an index showing what document is on which page. Use dividers between sections. Bring three copies: one for you, one for the commissioner, one for the employer. Organize chronologically (oldest to newest). Label exhibits clearly (Exhibit A, B, C). Practice referring to your bundle so you can find documents quickly during the hearing.

Witnesses

Witnesses can make or break your case. Prepare them carefully:

1

Choosing Witnesses

Select witnesses who saw what happened directly — not someone who heard about it second-hand. Colleagues who witnessed the dismissal, the disciplinary hearing, or your performance are ideal. Character witnesses (who say you are a "good person") are less useful than factual witnesses.

2

Preparing Your Witnesses

Meet with each witness before the hearing. Explain what will happen (oath, questions, cross-examination). Review their statement with them. Ensure they know what they can and cannot say. They must stick to facts they personally know. They cannot give legal opinions.

3

What Witnesses Can/Cannot Say

Can say: "I saw the manager shout at the employee on 15 March."
Cannot say: "I think the manager was unfair." (That's a legal conclusion)
Can say: "The disciplinary hearing lasted 10 minutes and the employee was not allowed to speak."
Cannot say: "The company always treats people badly." (Too general)

4

If Employer Witnesses Won't Attend

If you need a witness who works for the employer and they refuse to attend, you can request a subpoena from the CCMA. Apply at least 7 days before the hearing. The subpoena compels attendance. If the witness still refuses, the commissioner may draw an inference against the employer.

Witness Availability

Confirm with your witnesses the date, time, and venue. Give them your phone number. Ask them to arrive 30 minutes early. If a witness cannot attend, inform the CCMA immediately — you may be able to reschedule or use their written statement (with the commissioner's permission).

The Award

The Commissioner Decides

After the hearing, the commissioner issues a written award within 14 days. The award is a formal legal document that decides the winner and loser. It is binding and enforceable as if it were an order of the Labour Court.

14-Day Deadline

The commissioner must issue the award within 14 days of the hearing ending. If the matter is complex, they may request an extension, but this is rare.

Binding & Enforceable

The award is legally binding on both parties. If the employer does not comply, you can have the award made an order of the Labour Court and enforced by the sheriff.

Service of Award

The CCMA serves the award on both parties. The date you receive it is the service date — this starts the 6-week clock for a Labour Court review if you want to challenge it.

Written Reasons

If the award is brief, you may request written reasons within a short period. This helps you understand the commissioner's reasoning and decide whether to challenge the award.

If You Win

1

Award Enforcement

If the award orders reinstatement or compensation, the employer must comply. If they refuse, you can apply to the Labour Court to have the award made an order of court. The court can then enforce it via the sheriff (seizing assets, garnishing wages, etc.).

2

Payment Timelines

The award usually specifies a payment deadline (e.g., "within 14 days of the award"). If no deadline is specified, payment is due immediately. If the employer delays, contact the CCMA first, then the Labour Court if necessary.

3

What If the Employer Doesn't Pay?

First, send a formal written demand. If still no payment, apply to the Labour Court for enforcement. You may need to provide the employer's banking details or assets information. The sheriff can execute against the employer's property. You may need an attorney for enforcement.

Need Help Enforcing the Award?

Enforcement can be complex, especially if the employer resists. An attorney can help you navigate the Labour Court enforcement process.

If You Lose

6-Week Deadline — Do Not Miss It

You have 6 weeks from the date the award was served on you to challenge it in the Labour Court via a Section 145 review. This is a strict deadline. If you miss it, you may lose your right to challenge forever.

1

Section 145 Review

The Labour Relations Act allows you to challenge the award on three grounds: (1) Misconduct by the commissioner (bias, conflict of interest), (2) Gross irregularity in the proceedings (unfair procedure, excluded evidence), or (3) Excess of powers (the commissioner made a decision they had no legal authority to make).

2

Court Online Registration — Compulsory

All Labour Court matters must be filed via Court Online (courtonline.co.za). You must register, create an account, and file all documents electronically. Paper filing is not accepted for most matters. This is compulsory.

3

Condonation If Late

If you miss the 6-week deadline, you can apply for condonation — but this is risky and not guaranteed. You need "good cause" for the delay. Do not miss the deadline.

Challenging an Award Is Complex

A Section 145 review requires legal documents (Notice of Motion, Founding Affidavit), the arbitration record, and legal argument. This is the stage where most self-represented litigants need an attorney.
