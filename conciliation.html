IN-PERSON HEARING AT CCMA OFFICES

Prepare for Your Conciliation

You filed your CCMA referral (LRA Form 7.11). Now learn exactly what to expect, what to bring, and how to make informed decisions at your in-person conciliation hearing.

What is Conciliation?

Conciliation is the first formal step in resolving your workplace dispute. It is a compulsory, confidential, and without-prejudice process where a neutral CCMA commissioner helps you and your employer find common ground without escalating to arbitration or court. Without prejudiceMeans anything you say or offer during conciliation cannot be used against you later in arbitration or court if no settlement is reached.

The Goal

Reach a mutually acceptable settlement agreement. The commissioner does not impose a decision—you and your employer control the outcome.

The Commissioner

Acts as a neutral facilitator, not a judge. They clarify issues, explore solutions, and may meet with parties separately (caucus) or together.

Timeframe

The commissioner must attempt to resolve the dispute within 30 days from the date you referred it to the CCMA.

Not a Trial

No formal evidence is led under oath. You do not cross-examine witnesses. It is an informal, discussion-based process focused on settlement.

How Conciliation Differs from Arbitration & Court

Conciliation: Informal, voluntary settlement, you control the outcome, no evidence under oath, commissioner facilitates only.
Arbitration: Formal hearing, evidence under oath, witnesses testify, commissioner decides the winner and loser, binding award.
Labour Court: Formal litigation, legal representation standard, review of arbitration awards, typically longer and more expensive.

CCMA is IN-PERSON

Important: Physical Attendance Required

Conciliation hearings are held in person at CCMA offices (unless specifically arranged otherwise by the CCMA). You must attend physically. Failure to attend may result in your referral being deemed abandoned.

1

Arrival & Security

Arrive at least 30 minutes early. Bring your ID and your set-down notice. CCMA offices have security screening (bag checks, metal detectors). Allow extra time for queues. Dress professionally—business casual is appropriate.

2

Waiting Area

You will wait in a public waiting area until the commissioner calls you. You may see your employer or their representative here. Do not discuss your case with them in the waiting area—anything you say could be used later. Stay calm and review your notes.

3

The Hearing Room

The commissioner will escort you to a private hearing room. These are typically small conference rooms with a table and chairs. The setting is informal—not like a courtroom. The commissioner sits at the head, parties on opposite sides.

4

Duration

Conciliation can take anywhere from 30 minutes to several hours depending on the complexity of the dispute and willingness to negotiate. Schedule your entire day if possible. Bring water and snacks for the waiting period.

Recording for Personal Records

Under South African law (single-party consent), you may record conversations you are part of for your personal records. However, conciliation is confidential and without prejudice. Recording is for your memory aid only—you cannot use it to prove what was said in later proceedings unless all parties agree. Be discreet and respectful if you choose to record.

What to Bring — Checklist

Even though conciliation is informal, arriving with organized documents shows you are serious and helps you negotiate from a position of strength. Use this interactive checklist:

  • Original ID DocumentRequired for security and verification. Passport, driver's license, or smart ID card.
  • CCMA Set-Down NoticeThe official notice with your case number, date, time, and venue. You received this after filing Form 7.11.
  • Employment Contract & Job DescriptionProves your terms of employment, role, and responsibilities.
  • Payslips & Proof of EarningsLast 3–6 months of payslips and bank statements. Critical for calculating compensation or settlement amounts.
  • Dismissal Letter / Termination NoticeThe official document stating why you were dismissed. If verbal, bring any follow-up emails or messages confirming it.
  • Disciplinary Records & WarningsAny prior warnings, disciplinary hearing minutes, or performance improvement plans.
  • Witness Statements (if any)Written statements from colleagues who saw what happened. Witnesses do not typically testify at conciliation, but their statements support your position.
  • Evidence BundleOrganized copies of emails, WhatsApp messages, photos, or documents that support your case. Number your pages and bring a copy for the commissioner and one for the employer.
  • Pen, Paper & NotepadTake notes during the hearing. Write down settlement offers, deadlines, and next steps.
  • Water & SnacksWaiting times can be long. Stay hydrated and maintain your energy.
  • Settlement CalculationUse our calculator below to know your bottom line before you arrive. Know what you will accept and what you will reject.

Pro Tip: The "Conciliation Bundle"

While you don't need a full arbitration bundle, bring a simple 5-page summary: (1) Timeline of events, (2) Key documents, (3) Your desired outcome, (4) Minimum settlement you will accept, (5) List of strengths and weaknesses in your case. This keeps you focused during negotiations.

How to Behave at Conciliation

Professional Conduct

Dress neatly (business casual). Address the commissioner as "Commissioner" or "Sir/Madam." Stand when the commissioner enters the room. Turn your phone off completely. Do not interrupt when others are speaking.

Speaking to the Employer

Speak through the commissioner, not directly to the employer, unless the commissioner invites direct discussion. Never argue, shout, or make personal attacks. Stick to facts, not emotions. Say: "The facts show..." not "You always..."

Emotional Management

It is normal to feel angry or anxious. If you feel overwhelmed, ask the commissioner for a short break. Take deep breaths. Remember: conciliation is about negotiation, not confrontation. You can ask for a private caucus if emotions run high.

Ask for Clarification

If you do not understand a legal term or process, ask the commissioner to explain it in plain language. It is better to ask than to agree to something you do not understand. Say: "Commissioner, could you please explain what that means?"

What NOT to Do

Do not threaten the employer or commissioner. Do not bring unauthorized people into the hearing room (only you, your representative, or the parties listed in Rule 7.2 are permitted). Do not discuss the case in public waiting areas. Do not sign anything you have not read or do not understand.

Representation RulesAt conciliation, you may appear in person or be represented by a co-employee, a trade union official, or a registered employer's organisation official. Legal practitioners (attorneys/advocates) are generally NOT allowed to represent you at conciliation unless the commissioner and all parties agree. What to Expect During the Hearing

1

Introduction & Opening Statements

The commissioner introduces themselves and explains the process. They confirm the nature of the dispute. Both parties are invited to give a brief opening statement (5–10 minutes). State the facts simply: what happened, when, and what you want. Do not argue—just outline your position.

2

Issue Clarification

The commissioner identifies the key issues in dispute. If there is disagreement about the nature of the dispute, the commissioner may decide how to categorize it. This affects what happens next, so pay close attention.

3

Private Caucus (Separate Meetings)

The commissioner may meet with each party privately. This is called a caucusA private meeting between the commissioner and one party, without the other party present. What you say in caucus is confidential unless you authorize the commissioner to share it.. Be honest with the commissioner about your settlement range. They cannot force you to settle, but they can reality-test your position.

4

Settlement Discussions

The commissioner shuttles between rooms, conveying offers and counter-offers. This is where negotiation happens. Stay patient—this can take time. Do not feel rushed. Ask for time to think if you need it.

5

Commissioner's Role

The commissioner is neutral. They may suggest solutions or highlight weaknesses in your case (reality testing). They cannot give legal advice to either side. They cannot force a settlement. Their job is to help you reach your own agreement.

6

Confidentiality

Everything said during conciliation is confidential and without prejudice. If no settlement is reached, the commissioner cannot tell the arbitration commissioner what was discussed or offered. This allows open negotiation.

The Settlement Decision

Critical Warning: Don't Feel Pressured to Settle

You are NEVER required to settle. If the offer is not fair, you have the right to walk away. The commissioner may pressure you to compromise—that is their job. But the final decision is yours alone. Do not sign anything under duress.

How to Evaluate a Settlement Offer

Financial Reality

Calculate your actual losses: lost wages, benefits, pension contributions. Compare the offer to what you might win at arbitration (which is uncertain). A guaranteed smaller amount today may be better than a risky larger amount later.

Time & Stress

Arbitration can take months and causes significant stress. Consider whether the settlement offer compensates you fairly for the certainty and closure it provides.

Reinstatement

If you want your job back, is the employer offering reinstatement or re-employment? If not, is the compensation enough to bridge you to new employment?

Non-Financial Terms

Consider reference letters, confidentiality clauses, non-disparagement agreements, and payment timelines. A good reference may be worth more than extra money.

What to Ask For

  • Clear payment amount and deadline (e.g., "R50,000 within 14 days of signing")
  • UIF and tax clarity—who handles deductions?
  • Reference letter wording (get it attached to the agreement)
  • Reinstatement date and terms if applicable
  • Confidentiality—who can talk about the settlement?
  • What happens if the employer breaches the agreement?

When to Walk Away

Red Flags — Reject the Offer If:

The offer is significantly less than 3 months' wages without good reason. The payment terms are vague ("we'll pay when we can"). The employer refuses to put the agreement in writing. You feel bullied or the commissioner is pushing too hard. You have strong evidence and a high chance of winning at arbitration. You want reinstatement and the employer refuses to discuss it.

If Settlement is Reached

Congratulations — But Read Before You Sign

A settlement agreement is a legally binding contract. The commissioner will draft it and both parties must sign. Once signed, the dispute is resolved and you generally cannot take it further.

Key Settlement Agreement Terms

Payment Terms

Exact amount, payment date, and method. Include a penalty clause for late payment (e.g., interest at prime rate). Specify if it is gross or net of tax.

Confidentiality

Who must keep the settlement secret? Often mutual. Ensure you understand if you can tell family or future employers. Get a written reference attached.

Binding Nature

The agreement is enforceable as if it were an arbitration award. If the employer breaches it, you can have it made an order of the Labour Court and enforce via the sheriff.

Non-Admission

Most settlements state the employer "does not admit liability." This is standard. It means they are paying to resolve the dispute, not admitting they broke the law.

Before You Leave the CCMA Office

Ensure you receive a signed copy of the settlement agreement before you leave. Check that all pages are initialed and the last page is fully signed by both parties and the commissioner. Verify the case number is correct. Take a photo of the signed agreement with your phone as backup.

If No Settlement is Reached

If you and your employer cannot agree, the commissioner will issue a Certificate of Non-ResolutionAlso called a "Certificate of Outcome" or "CNR." It is an official document stating the dispute remains unresolved after conciliation. It is required to proceed to the next step.. This is not a loss—it simply means the dispute moves to the next phase.

1

Certificate of Non-Resolution Issued

The commissioner will issue this certificate immediately or send it within a few days. It is sent to both parties. Keep this document safe—you cannot proceed without it.

2

Next Step: Arbitration (Form 7.13)

For most disputes (unfair dismissal, unfair labour practice, individual retrenchment), you must complete LRA Form 7.13 to request arbitration. This must be done within the timeframes specified by the CCMA (usually 90 days from the certificate date, but check your certificate).

3

Labour Court Path

For automatically unfair dismissals, discrimination claims above the BCEA threshold, or retrenchment disputes, the certificate allows you to proceed to the Labour Court instead of arbitration. These paths are more complex and legal representation is strongly advised.

Watch Your Deadlines

Missing the deadline to request arbitration or file in the Labour Court can result in your dispute being dismissed. Mark the dates on your calendar as soon as you receive the certificate. If you are late, you may need a condonation application with good cause.

Con-Arb Warning

If con-arb (conciliation-arbitration) was not objected to when the referral was filed, arbitration may follow immediately if conciliation fails. This means you could go straight from settlement talks into a formal hearing on the same day.

Be Prepared for Both:

  • Bring all evidence and witnesses as if arbitration is happening today
  • Dress and prepare for a formal hearing, not just a chat
  • If you are not ready for con-arb, you may request a postponement, but this is not guaranteed
  • Employers often object to con-arb—check your set-down notice carefully
